Critical Waiting room Wi-Fi

Guest Wi-Fi can reach the Practice Wi-Fi network

From Waiting room Wi-Fi, 2 devices on Practice Wi-Fi (192.168.10.0/24) answered.

Why it matters

Anyone using the guest Wi-Fi can reach your business computers, as if they had plugged into the office network.

How to fix it

Separate the networks: put guest Wi-Fi on its own VLAN or the router's guest mode, and block guest-to-staff traffic in the firewall.

On your TP-Link router

Usually under Advanced › Guest Network: turn the guest network on and untick “Allow guests to see each other and access my local network”. Menus vary by model and firmware: TP-Link support has the guide for yours.

High Waiting room Wi-Fi

The router lets devices open ports to the internet (UPnP) from guest Wi-Fi

The router at 192.168.50.1 answered a UPnP search and offers the port-forwarding service.

Why it matters

Any device on this network, including malware or a guest's laptop, can ask the router to expose itself to the whole internet, without a password and without anyone noticing.

How to fix it

Turn UPnP off in the router's settings. If a game console or camera needs a port open, add a manual port-forward for that one device.

On your TP-Link router

Usually under Advanced › NAT Forwarding › UPnP: switch it off. Menus vary by model and firmware: TP-Link support has the guide for yours.

Staff Wi-Fi relies on one shared password

Practice Wi-Fi uses WPA2 Personal.

Why it matters

Everyone shares one password, so former staff and visitors who were told it can still join, and a captured handshake can be cracked offline if the password is weak.

How to fix it

Use a long random password and change it when staff leave. Move to WPA3, or WPA2/WPA3 Enterprise with individual logins.

Method and scope

How we checked

What we did

  • Joined each network as an ordinary user would.
  • On staff networks, asked devices for their names (mDNS), as a phone or laptop does when it looks for a printer.
  • Looked for devices that answer, at no more than 50 packets a second.
  • Checked the 100 most common ports on devices the business owns.
  • On guest Wi-Fi, counted other people’s devices without probing them, and tested whether guests can reach the business networks.
  • Asked each router, with a single discovery message, whether it lets devices open ports to the internet (UPnP).
  • Looked up the software versions we saw in public vulnerability databases.
  • Kept a sealed record of every command and its raw output.

What we did not do

  • Guess or try any password.
  • Run exploits or change any setting.
  • Capture or read anyone’s traffic.
  • Touch anything outside the ranges listed in the signed scope.
  • Record names or hardware addresses of guests’ devices.

Limits of this check

This is a snapshot of 27 November 2026. Devices that were off or asleep would not show up. We checked common TCP ports, not every port, and of the UDP services only UPnP. Software versions that devices reported were matched against NVD, CISA’s Known Exploited Vulnerabilities list and FIRST’s exploit scores on 27 November 2026; only product and version names were sent, never addresses. Matching by version can over-report when a maker fixes a flaw without changing the version number, and 1 service(s) didn’t report a version precise enough to check. A clean result here doesn’t cover email, cloud accounts or the internet-facing side of the network.
